TYPES OF DATA ANALYSIS

Academic Module | Data Science & Analytics | 30-Page Comprehensive Guide

A structured learning module covering the major approaches used to understand, explain, predict, and support decisions from data.

1. Introduction to Data Analysis

Overview: Data analysis is the systematic process of collecting, cleaning, transforming, exploring, interpreting, and communicating data to answer questions and support decisions. It converts raw observations into useful information and, ultimately, actionable knowledge.

Why Data Analysis Matters: Organizations generate data from transactions, sensors, surveys, websites, applications, experiments, and social platforms. Analysis helps identify patterns, measure performance, understand customers, evaluate hypotheses, detect problems, and predict future outcomes.

Basic Analysis Cycle: A typical workflow is: define the problem → collect data → clean data → explore data → apply analytical methods → interpret results → communicate findings → take action.

Example: A university can analyze attendance, assessment scores, and course feedback to identify subjects where students need additional support.

2. Major Types of Data Analysis

Main Categories: Data analysis is commonly divided into descriptive, diagnostic, exploratory, inferential, predictive, and prescriptive analysis. Different classifications also include causal, qualitative, quantitative, and real-time analysis.

TypeMain QuestionTypical Output
DescriptiveWhat happened?Reports, summaries, dashboards
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Drivers, relationships, root causes
ExploratoryWhat patterns exist?Trends, clusters, anomalies
InferentialWhat can we conclude about a population?Estimates, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests
PredictiveWhat is likely to happen?Forecasts, probabilities, risk scores
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Recommendations, optimized actions

Important Point: These types are not mutually exclusive. A real project may use several methods in sequence.

3. Descriptive Data Analysis

Definition: Descriptive analysis summarizes historical or current data so that its important characteristics can be understood quickly. It does not primarily attempt to explain causation or predict the future.

Common Techniques: Counts, percentages, totals, averages, medians, minimum and maximum values, frequency distributions, cross-tabulations, and time-based summaries.

Example: For a class of 100 students, descriptive analysis can report average marks, pass percentage, highest score, lowest score, and attendance distribution.

Advantages: Easy to understand, useful for reporting, and provides the foundation for more advanced analysis.

4. Diagnostic Data Analysis

Definition: Diagnostic analysis investigates reasons behind observed outcomes. It moves beyond 'what happened?' to ask 'why did it happen?'

Methods: Drill-down analysis, comparison of segments, correlation analysis, root-cause analysis, variance analysis, and examination of contributing factors.

Example: If sales decreased in one quarter, analysts may compare regions, products, prices, promotions, inventory levels, and customer segments to identify likely causes.

Caution: Association does not automatically establish causation. A relationship between two variables may be caused by another factor.

5. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

Definition: EDA is an open-ended investigation of data intended to discover patterns, relationships, unusual observations, and useful questions before formal modeling.

Typical Activities: Inspecting distributions, plotting variables, checking missing values, examining correlations, identifying outliers, and comparing groups.

Why It Is Important: EDA can reveal data-quality problems and unexpected patterns that would otherwise produce misleading models.

Example: A histogram of delivery times may reveal a long right tail, while a box plot may identify unusually delayed orders.

6. Inferential Data Analysis

Definition: Inferential analysis uses a sample to draw conclusions about a larger population while accounting for uncertainty.

Key Concepts: Sampling distributions, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, p-values, standard errors, and statistical significance.

Example: A university surveys 300 students and uses the sample to estimate the proportion of all students who prefer online learning.

Limitation: Inference depends on appropriate sampling, assumptions, measurement quality, and correct statistical procedures.

7. Predictive Data Analysis

Definition: Predictive analysis uses historical data, statistical methods, or machine learning to estimate future or unknown outcomes.

Applications: Demand forecasting, credit-risk scoring, customer churn prediction, fraud detection, equipment failure prediction, and recommendation systems.

Methods: Regression, classification, time-series forecasting, decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting, and neural networks.

8. Prescriptive Data Analysis

Definition: Prescriptive analysis recommends actions by combining predictions, constraints, objectives, and possible decisions.

Typical Methods: Optimization, simulation, decision analysis, scenario analysis, and rule-based recommendation systems.

Difference: Predictive analysis estimates what may happen; prescriptive analysis considers what action should be taken.

9. Qualitative vs Quantitative Analysis

Qualitative: Examines non-numerical information such as interview transcripts, observations, survey text, and feedback using coding, thematic, and content analysis.

Quantitative: Works with numerical data and applies mathematical and statistical techniques to measure relationships, differences, trends, and uncertainty.

10. Univariate, Bivariate & Multivariate Analysis

  • Univariate: Studies one variable at a time (mean, median, range, variance, histograms).
  • Bivariate: Examines two variables together to understand association (correlation, scatter plots, cross-tabulation).
  • Multivariate: Examines multiple variables simultaneously (multiple regression, PCA, clustering, MANOVA).

11. Time-Series, Correlation & Regression

Time-Series: Studies temporal observations to identify trend, seasonality, cyclical fluctuations, and noise using moving averages, ARIMA, or exponential smoothing.

Correlation: Measures the degree and direction of linear association between variables. Correlation does not imply causation.

Regression: Models the functional relationship between an outcome variable and one or more explanatory variables for forecasting and impact evaluation.

12. Machine Learning: Classification & Clustering

Classification: Supervised task assigning observations to predefined categories (decision trees, logistic regression, random forests, SVMs).

Clustering: Unsupervised grouping of observations based on similarity (k-means, hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN).

13. Summary & Best Practices

Best Practices: Define the analytical question clearly, clean and validate data before modeling, select methods based on the objective, verify assumptions, quantify uncertainty, avoid unsupported causal claims, and communicate results honestly with intuitive visualizations.
